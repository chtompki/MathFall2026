    Identify how the phase portrait is similar to or different from the vector field.

    \begin{solution}
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item[(a)] We factor the right-hand side:
            \[
                \dot x=x-x^2=x(1-x).
            \]
            Thus the equilibria are $x=0$ and $x=1$. The sign of $\dot x$ on the
            three complementary intervals is
            \[
                \begin{array}{c|ccc}
                    x&(-\infty,0)&(0,1)&(1,\infty)\\ \hline
                    x(1-x)&-&+&-
                \end{array}.
            \]
            Therefore the phase line is
            \[
                \textstyle
                (-\infty)\ \longleftarrow\ \boxed{0}\ \longrightarrow\
                \boxed{1}\ \longleftarrow\ (+\infty).
            \]
            The equilibrium $x=0$ is unstable because arrows point away from it,
            while $x=1$ is asymptotically stable because arrows point toward it.

            The vector field and the phase portrait contain the same directional
            information: the sign of $f(x)$ tells whether motion is to the left or
            right, and the zeros of $f$ give the equilibria. The vector-field plot
            displays a vector at each sampled point, often with its length reflecting
            $|f(x)|$. The phase portrait emphasizes the solution orbits and their
            direction, so arrow lengths are not essential.

            In one dimension, trajectories lie on the phase line. A solution beginning
            in $0<x<1$ moves monotonically right toward $1$, and a solution beginning
            with $x>1$ moves monotonically left toward $1$. A solution beginning with
            $x<0$ moves left, away from $0$. The two equilibrium trajectories remain
            fixed at $x=0$ and $x=1$. Non-equilibrium trajectories cannot cross either
            one. For initial value $x(0)=x_0$, the non-equilibrium solutions can also be
            written
            \[
                x(t)=\frac{x_0e^t}{1+x_0(e^t-1)}
            \]
            on their maximal intervals of existence.

            \item[(b)] In the displayed pair, the \textbf{left-hand image is the
            vector field}: it consists of many individual arrows showing the velocity
            assigned at sampled points. The \textbf{right-hand image is the phase
            portrait}: it shows solution curves whose tangent vectors agree with the
            vector field.

            To verify the qualitative shape, write the system as
            \[
                \binom{\dot x}{\dot y}
                =A\binom{x}{y},
                \qquad
                A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.
            \]
            Its characteristic polynomial and eigenvalues are
            \[
                \det(A-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)^2+6,
                \qquad \lambda=1\pm i\sqrt6.
            \]
            Because the real part is positive, the origin is an unstable spiral: nearby
            nonzero trajectories spiral outward as time increases. At $(1,0)$ the
            velocity is $(1,3)$, which points upward, so the rotation is
            counterclockwise. This agrees with the right-hand phase portrait.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{solution}

        \item (Generic 2d system of linear differential equations)

        \emph{Rotate roles: questioner $\to$ timekeeper $\to$ scribe}

        Consider the system
        \[\sys{\dfrac{dx}{dt}=ax+by}{\dfrac{dy}{dt}=cx+dy}\]
        with fixed point at the origin.
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Rewrite this in matrix / vector form (let $\vct{x}=\binom{x}{y}$.)
            \item Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. Recall that the eigenvalues of $A$, $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, are given by $\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta=0$ where $\tau=a+d$ is the trace of the matrix $A$ and $\Delta=ad-bc$ is the determinant of the matrix $A$. In addition, $(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)=0$. Why?
            \item Use this second fact (and match terms in the two equations) to show that $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\tau$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2=\Delta$.
            \item Consider the $\Delta\tau$-plane (with $\Delta$ on the horizontal axis). Identify regions of the $\Delta\tau$ plane where the matrix has two negative eigenvalues, regions where it has one positive eigenvalue and one negative one, and regions where it has two positive eigenvalues.
            \item Consider $\vct{v}e^{\lambda_1t}$, where $\lambda_1$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ and $\vct{v}$ is the associated eigenvector. For $\lambda_1$ a real number, why is it a straight-line solution?
            \item General solutions to this differential equation are of the form $\vct{x}(t)=c_1\vct{v}_1e^{\lambda_1t}+c_2\vct{v}_2e^{\lambda_2t}$. We might have $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ both real. Or they could be a complex conjugate pair, where

    \end{enumerate}

    $\lambda_1=\lambda+i\omega$ and $\lambda_2=\lambda-i\omega$. When they are a complex conjugate pair, we require the solution to the differential equation to be real, so we require $c_1$ and $c_2$ to be a complex conjugate pair as well.

    For the systems
    \[
        \sys{\dot x=x}{\dot y=x-y}\qquad
        \sys{\dot x=-x-y}{\dot y=x-2y}\qquad
        \sys{\dot x=x}{\dot y=-y}\qquad
        \sys{\dot x=x+y}{\dot y=-2x+y}
    \]
    find their trace and their determinant. Which have real eigenvalues and which have eigenvalues that are a complex conjugate pair?

    \hspace{2em}(g)\quad Attempt to match the systems above to the phase portraits below.
    \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=.76\textwidth]{four-portraits.png}\end{center}

    \begin{solution}
        Write
        \[
            \vct{x}=\binom{x}{y},\qquad
            A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.
        \]

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item[(a)] The two scalar equations combine into
            \[
                \boxed{\frac{d\vct{x}}{dt}=A\vct{x}},\qquad
                \frac{d}{dt}\binom{x}{y}
                =\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\binom{x}{y}.
            \]

            \item[(b)] An eigenvalue is, by definition, a value of $\lambda$ for
            which $A-\lambda I$ is singular. Therefore the eigenvalues are precisely
            the roots of the characteristic polynomial
            \[
                p(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)
                =\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta.
            \]
            A monic quadratic with roots $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ factors as
            \[
                p(\lambda)=(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2),
            \]
            so the equation $p(\lambda)=0$ is equivalently
            $(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)=0$.

            \item[(c)] Expanding the factored form gives
            \[
                (\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)
                =\lambda^2-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda
                +\lambda_1\lambda_2.
            \]
            Comparing this with $\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta$ yields
            \[
                \boxed{\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\tau},\qquad
                \boxed{\lambda_1\lambda_2=\Delta}.
            \]

            \item[(d)] The eigenvalues are
            \[
                \lambda_{1,2}=\frac{\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}}{2}.
            \]
            Thus the parabola $\Delta=\tau^2/4$ separates real eigenvalues from
            complex conjugate eigenvalues. The requested real-eigenvalue regions are
            \[
                \begin{array}{c|c}
                    \text{region in the }(\Delta,\tau)\text{-plane}&\text{eigenvalue signs}\\ \hline
                    \Delta<0&\text{one positive and one negative}\\
                    0<\Delta\leq \tau^2/4,\ \tau>0&\text{two positive}\\
                    0<\Delta\leq \tau^2/4,\ \tau<0&\text{two negative}
                \end{array}
            \]
            On $\Delta=0$, one eigenvalue is zero. Above the parabola,
            $\Delta>\tau^2/4$, the eigenvalues are complex and have real part
            $\tau/2$. Equality on the parabola gives a repeated real eigenvalue.

            \item[(e)] Since $A\vct{v}=\lambda_1\vct{v}$,
            \[
                \frac{d}{dt}\left(\vct{v}e^{\lambda_1t}\right)
                =\lambda_1\vct{v}e^{\lambda_1t}
                =(A\vct{v})e^{\lambda_1t}
                =A\left(\vct{v}e^{\lambda_1t}\right).
            \]
            Hence $\vct{x}(t)=\vct{v}e^{\lambda_1t}$ solves
            $\dot{\vct{x}}=A\vct{x}$. If $\lambda_1$ is real, every point of the
            solution is a scalar multiple of the fixed vector $\vct{v}$. Its trajectory
            therefore lies on the straight line $\operatorname{span}\{\vct{v}\}$.
            When $\lambda_1<0$ it approaches the origin in forward time; when
            $\lambda_1>0$ it moves away from the origin.

            \item[(f)] For each displayed system, form its coefficient matrix and use
            $D=\tau^2-4\Delta$:
            \[
                \begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
                    \text{system}&A&\tau&\Delta&\lambda_{1,2}\\ \hline
                    1&\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}&0&-1&1,-1\\[6pt]
                    2&\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}&-3&3&
                    \dfrac{-3\pm i\sqrt3}{2}\\[8pt]
                    3&\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}&0&-1&1,-1\\[6pt]
                    4&\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}&2&3&1\pm i\sqrt2
                \end{array}
            \]
            Systems 1 and 3 have real eigenvalues of opposite sign, so both are
            saddles. System 2 has a complex conjugate pair with negative real part, so
            it is a stable spiral. System 4 has a complex conjugate pair with positive
            real part, so it is an unstable spiral.

            \item[(g)] The matches, reading the portraits from left to right and then
            top to bottom, are
            \[
                \begin{array}{c|c|l}
                    \text{portrait}&\text{system}&\text{reason}\\ \hline
                    \text{upper left}&3&\text{axis-aligned saddle}\\
                    \text{upper right}&1&\text{tilted saddle; unstable line }y=x/2\\
                    \text{lower left}&4&\text{clockwise unstable spiral}\\
                    \text{lower right}&2&\text{counterclockwise stable spiral}
                \end{array}
            \]
            For system 1, the stable eigendirection is the $y$-axis and the unstable
            eigendirection is $y=x/2$, producing the tilted saddle. For system 3, both
            eigendirections are coordinate axes. To distinguish the spirals, examine
            the vector at $(1,0)$: system 2 gives $(-1,1)$, which points inward and
            counterclockwise, while system 4 gives $(1,-2)$, which points outward and
            clockwise.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{solution}

    \textbf{Answers:} 2a: $\vct{x}=\binom{x}{y}$, $\dot{\vct{x}}=\dfrac d{dt}\binom{x}{y}$, $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. The equation becomes $\dot{\vct{x}}=A\vct{x}$.

    2b: $(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)=0$ because the eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are the roots of the characteristic equation.

    2c: Expanding, $\lambda^2-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda+\lambda_1\lambda_2=0$. We have $\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta$ and $\lambda^2-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda+\lambda_1\lambda_2$. These polynomials have the same roots and the same leading coefficient: they are the same polynomial. So $\tau=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$ and $\Delta=\lambda_1\lambda_2$.

    2d: The left side has $\Delta<0$ so one positive and one negative. The first quadrant has two positive eigenvalues. The fourth quadrant has two negative.

    \scriptsize
    2e: $\vct{x}=\vct{v}e^{\lambda_1t}$. $\dot{\vct{x}}=\vct{v}\lambda_1e^{\lambda_1t}$. $A\vct{x}=A\vct{v}e^{\lambda_1t}$. $\vct{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ so $A\vct{v}=\lambda_1\vct{v}$. The sides match. This is a straight line solution because the solution is a constant multiple of a single vector direction, so we move along that direction (either exponential growth, or exponential decay) as time increases.

    2f:
    \[
        \begin{array}{c|l|l}
            A&\tau&\Delta\text{ and eigenvalues}\\\hline
            \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}&1+(-1)=0&1(-1)-0(1)=-1<0,\ \text{real}\\[6pt]
            \begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}&-1+(-2)=-3&2-(-1)=3,\ \tau^2-4\Delta=-3,\ \text{complex}\\[6pt]
            \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}&1+(-1)=0&1(-1)-0(0)=-1<0,\ \text{real}\\[6pt]
            \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}&1+1=2&1(1)-1(-2)=3,\ \tau^2-4\Delta=-8,\ \text{complex}
        \end{array}
    \]
    where $\lambda_\pm=\tau/2\pm\frac12\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}$.

    2g: $\sys{\dot x=x}{\dot y=-y}$ has eigenvectors along the axes so matches to the upper left plot.

    $\sys{\dot x=x}{\dot y=x-y}$ is the other saddle point so matches to the upper right plot.
